Concepts

A heatmap is not a photograph of your radio field. It is built from a handful of measurements, some geometry and a model, and each part deserves a different amount of trust. This chapter explains what the numbers are made of, what dBm means, and where the tool is allowed to be wrong.

v0.1~11 min read

From a reading to a map

Every path starts with one HTTP request. A scanner node collects a 15-second burst of scans, reduces it to one value per BSSID, and posts it to POST /api/v1/readings with its node token. The server validates it, writes a row to SQLite and returns; nothing is computed at ingest.

The engine works when you ask for a view: it reads the readings for that floor, then runs three calculations — interpolation across your measured points, a propagation estimate from the AP and the walls, and a kNN match against positions you already recorded. The browser receives a grid of values and paints it. In v0.1 only the first half exists: readings are validated and written to SQLite; the calculations described in this chapter are planned.

That separation matters when you debug: if the point count does not increase, the problem sits between the node and the API; if it does, the problem is the engine or the points.

Measured, interpolated, modelled

The viewer mixes three kinds of value, and they are not interchangeable.

measured A number you stood still for

One sample at one position: the RSSI your adapter reported per BSSID during 15 seconds of scans. The only value that is not an estimate.

interpolated A guess between your points

IDW or kriging between measured points on the same floor. Accurate near a sample, progressively vaguer in the middle of a large empty room.

modelled Physics plus your walls

A log-distance path loss model with per-material wall losses, calibrated to your data. Covers the whole floor, at 4–7 dB RMSE.

The blended view is a weighted merge: interpolated near your points, modelled further away. It looks complete because it is complete, but a blended cell in an unvisited corner is mostly model.

Why insist on the difference? The action depends on it. A weak measured point is a fact: that corner is bad. A weak interpolated cell may be a real dead spot or a gap between samples. A weak modelled cell is a prediction that deserves a walk before you move an AP.

Label in the UIWhat produced itHow much to trust it
measuredA reading you posted at that position.High, within the device's own ±1–2 dB variance.
interpolatedIDW or kriging between measured points.High within 2 m of a sample, weak beyond 6 m.

What dBm actually means

RSSI is reported in dBm, a logarithmic ratio to one milliwatt. The numbers do not behave like intuition: −60 dBm is a thousand times more power than −90 dBm, yet the step from −70 to −60 is the same size as −40 to −30.

RSSIIn practiceWhat still works
−30 dBmExcellent — next to the APEverything, at the highest rates.
−50 dBmVery strongEverything; typical of the same room.
−60 dBmGoodHigh throughput, stable latency.
−67 dBmGood for video callsReal-time voice and video without artefacts.
−75 dBmUsableWeb, mail, streaming; calls stutter under load.
−80 dBmMarginalBackground sync only. Roaming gets unreliable here.
−90 dBmUnusableAssociation may succeed; nothing transfers reliably.

The 3 dB rule does most of the work: every 3 dB is a factor of two in power, so a wall that costs 3 dB halves the signal. In free space 6 dB roughly halves the usable range. Indoors the exponent is higher, so a 3 dB wall can cost more than a third of the remaining range.

Band choice is a trade-off you cannot win outright:

  • 2.4 GHz reaches further and penetrates walls better. It has three non-overlapping channels (1, 6, 11) at 20 MHz, and shares the band with Bluetooth, microwaves and every neighbour.
  • 5 GHz starts about 6 dB weaker at one metre (40.2 dB versus 46.4 dB of free-space loss) and loses more per wall, but has far more spectrum and much less interference.

So: 5 GHz for throughput in the same room, 2.4 GHz for reach and for devices that cannot do anything else.

Accuracy you can expect

These are the numbers the tool is designed around, not a guarantee for your building.

What you are looking atTypical errorWhy
A measured point, repeated at the same spot±1–2 dBFading, orientation and body absorption; the device's own driver smoothing.
An interpolated cell 3–6 m from the nearest sample±5 dBWalls and furniture between your samples are invisible to the interpolator.
A model estimate anywhere on the floor4–7 dB RMSEHeld-out error from 5-fold cross-validation on your own points.
A live position estimate2–4 mkNN over per-BSSID fingerprints, smoothed with an EMA; refuses below 3 shared BSSIDs.
A reading taken through nmcli±3 dBNetworkManager reports 0–100 %, which is coarser than dBm.

Three consequences. A 5 dB difference between adjacent cells is inside the noise floor; a 15 dB difference is real. And a model reporting 4 dB RMSE is not automatically better than one reporting 6 dB on fewer points — check the point count first.

Where this is honest about its limits

  • One device, one antenna pattern. Your laptop's radio is not your phone's. A survey measures the surveying device; other clients differ by several dB.
  • No spectrum analysis. The tool reads what your adapter can see. It cannot show a non-Wi-Fi interferer, and it cannot separate co-channel load from signal strength.
  • One moment in time. A reading is 15 seconds, and doors, people, furniture, DFS events and neighbour traffic all move the numbers afterwards.
  • Interpolation is not measurement. Large open rooms and thick walls are where interpolation is weakest, and where people ask it the hardest questions.
  • Model calibration is only as good as the spread. Points in a line, or all in one room, produce a confident model that is wrong everywhere else.
  • Position needs a fingerprint. Fewer than three shared BSSIDs, or a floor with no survey points, and the UI says it cannot estimate instead of guessing.

Known limitation

This is not a certified site survey. It does not replace a calibrated survey kit for compliance or a business SLA, and it cannot measure throughput, latency or airtime. It answers "where is coverage weak and why" on your own hardware.

Colour is not a verdict

The palette shows relative strength, not pass or fail. A room at −72 dBm can be fine for a laptop and unusable for a doorbell. Pick a threshold per device class, then read the numbers.

Glossary

TermMeaning
RSSIReceived signal strength, in dBm. Negative, and closer to zero is stronger.
BSSIDThe MAC address of one radio on one AP. A dual-band AP has at least two, one per band.
SSIDThe network name clients see. Several BSSIDs can carry the same SSID.
Channel width20, 40, 80 or 160 MHz. Wider is faster per client but occupies more spectrum and hears more neighbours.
Co-channel interferenceTwo APs on the same channel sharing airtime. Shows up as good RSSI with bad throughput.
IDWInverse distance weighting: each measured point contributes in proportion to 1/d². Fast, local, and it cannot extrapolate sensibly.
KrigingInterpolation with a fitted variogram, here a spherical model with an 8 m range. Smoother than IDW and it reports its own uncertainty.
FingerprintThe vector of RSSI values per BSSID at a known position. Position estimation matches the live vector against stored ones.
Held-out RMSERoot mean square error on points excluded from calibration, averaged over 5 folds. The model's honest self-assessment.
Sticky clientA device that stays associated with a distant AP instead of roaming to a closer one. Visible as a strong nearby AP and a weak serving AP.
