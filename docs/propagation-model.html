Propagation model

A heatmap only knows where you walked. The model fills in the rest: it uses your building's geometry, the materials between each access point and each point, and the survey points you collected, to estimate signal anywhere on the plan.

v0.1~6 min read

Planned — not in v0.1

Propagation modelling and calibration are planned. v0.1 has no path-loss fit, no wall multipliers, no held-out RMSE and no what-if preview. The chapter describes the model the release will fit once survey data exists.

An estimate that works where you did not measure

A survey point is one measurement, at one spot, at one moment. You will visit tens of them; the floor has hundreds. The model sits between them — a physical calculation, not an interpolation — which is why it can answer what a heatmap cannot: what if I move this access point two metres left, or add a third one upstairs?

It is a log-distance path-loss model with explicit wall losses.

PL(d) = PL(1 m) + 10 · n · log10(d) + Σ (L_i × k_i)

PL(1 m) = 40.2 dB   at 2.4 GHz
PL(1 m) = 46.4 dB   at 5 GHz
n       = 2.7       default, clamped to 2.0 – 4.0

PL(1 m)Reference loss at one metre40.2 dB at 2.4 GHz, 46.4 at 5 GHz

The exponent sets how fast signal falls with distance. In free space (n = 2) each doubling costs 6 dB; at the default 2.7, about 8.1 dB — close to the 8–9 dB a house with internal walls shows. Calibration adjusts it first.

Walls, doorways and the path search

The obvious implementation — count the walls a straight line from AP to receiver crosses — is wrong often enough to matter. The model builds a 2D navigation mesh per floor instead: one node per room, one edge per doorway, then an A* search from the AP's room to the room being evaluated. Mesh edges come from the walls you confirmed during model import, so a missing wall makes every path through it free.

Each edge costs the distance loss for its segment plus the loss of the boundary it crosses. A doorway edge costs 2 dB whatever the wall around it is made of: a path from the hallway into the kitchen through an open door should not pay the 10 dB of the brick wall the door sits in, and A* routes through the cheaper opening.

A straight-line count also fails because a diagonal path can cross the same wall twice: a wall down the middle of a floor, a corridor that doubles back, an L-shaped room. Counting unique surfaces undercounts; counting crossings on the real path does not.

Calibration

Three things are fitted: the exponent n, a multiplier per material, and a transmit-power offset per access point. The offset absorbs the fact that two APs at the same configured power rarely radiate the same amount.

The fit is ordinary least squares over every survey point, minimising the squared difference between predicted and measured RSSI. Multipliers are clamped to 0.5–1.5 so a thin data set cannot turn drywall into lead, and n to 2.0–4.0. A good fit leaves residuals scattered either side of zero, no room consistently 10 dB off, and a held-out error of 4–7 dB.

Overfitting is real on small surveys: below roughly 15 points per band there is more freedom in the parameters than data to constrain them, and the fit starts explaining noise. The UI marks such a fit unreliable and holds parameters near their defaults; twenty to twenty-five points per floor is where it becomes worth trusting.

Held-out error

A model scored on its own training points always looks good. Wi-Fi Survey reports 5-fold cross-validation: points are split into five groups, the model is fitted on four and scored on the fifth, and the rotation repeats until every point has been predicted by a model that never saw it. RMSE and MAE are both shown — RMSE punishes the occasional large miss, MAE describes the typical one.

An RMSE of 4 dB means the model is usually within half a wall of the truth: good enough to judge coverage shape and compare placements. At 8 dB it is guessing — trust the measured heatmap and add points before concluding anything. The score is broken down per band; 5 GHz is more directional and more absorbed, and usually scores worse.

It sits next to the map, not buried in a settings panel, because it is the confidence interval for everything else on screen.

Residual correction

After the global fit there is always structure left in the error. The model calls a partition drywall at 3 dB; the partition has metal studs and a foil facing, so every path through it is 6 dB optimistic. No global parameter can fix that without breaking every other wall.

So the leftover per-point error is smoothed into a bias surface with inverse-distance weighting at a 6 m radius and added back on top. It varies smoothly across the floor and per AP: near the metal-stud wall it pushes predictions down, in the open hallway it does nothing. It absorbs what the material list cannot know — foil-backed insulation, a chimney breast, a steel beam, a metal door frame.

What it cannot fix is bad geometry. An AP drawn three metres from where it really is produces not a smooth bias but a wrong distance, and smoothing that gives a plausible surface that is wrong in the same way. Fix the geometry, then recalibrate.

What-if previews

With a calibrated model you can change the plan without touching anything physical; the preview recomputes the floor in a second or two.

  • Move an AP — every distance and wall crossing is recalculated.
  • Add an AP — combined with the existing radios by the same per-band rules as a survey.
  • Change power — shifts that AP's offset, moving its footprint up or down.
  • Change channel — switches reference curve and wall losses to the other band.

Every preview is labelled model, not measured and drawn in a different ramp from a measured heatmap. Pin two scenarios to compare them side by side, with a delta view showing where coverage improves and degrades. It is still an estimate, so the step after a promising preview is to move the AP and survey again.

The doorway row surprises people: walking through an opening costs 2 dB whether the surrounding wall is plasterboard or brick. That is a simplification, since a thick stone opening attenuates more, but closer to the truth than charging the full wall loss.

What the model cannot see

The model knows walls and distance. It does not know furniture — a full bookshelf or tiled bathroom will surprise it — or people, and a room with thirty of them is several dB worse than the empty one you surveyed. It treats each AP as a single radiator, so MIMO, beamforming and antenna downtilt are invisible. It has no notion of interference or noise floor, so it can promise coverage where the real problem is a neighbour's network. There is no 6 GHz support.

Model, not measurement

A preview says what the calibrated model expects after a change, not what your hardware will do: antenna patterns, firmware power tables and interference sit outside the model. Use it to rule options out, then measure what you keep. Preview numbers are estimates, not survey results.

If you need certainty about a corner, put a survey point there; the model exists to tell you which corners are worth walking to. See Heatmaps for the measured side and Live position for what the same fingerprints do once collected.
